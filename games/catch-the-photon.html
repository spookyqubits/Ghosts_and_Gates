How to play

Goal. Make your detector bright and your opponent's dark, then catch the photon.

Cards. The four suits are four waves: ♠ starts at a crest, ♥ peaks a quarter later, ♣ starts at a trough, ♦ peaks three quarters later. Card numbers do not matter. Same color, opposite suit (♠ and ♣, or ♥ and ♦) cancel. Black with red: a quarter-step apart.

Each round (8 rounds). Both players secretly choose one card and place it face-down on a detector: their own (to brighten it) or their opponent's (to cancel it). Reveal both. Each card moves that detector's token one square: ♠ up, ♣ down, ♥ right, ♦ left. The square shows the real summed wave, and its number is the brightness.

Catch the photon. After 8 rounds, fire the photon. It lands at a random spot on the bar, and each detector's share of the bar equals its share of the total brightness. If both detectors are dark (0), play one more round.

Passing one screen. Before each turn, the other player looks away, then the player clicks "Show my cards". On a projector, use "Open hands" so the class can discuss each move, or tick "Computer plays B".

Edge of the board. A card cannot push a token off the board. If both cards push the same token off the edge in one round, the second card is lost.

Why it works

Every wave can be drawn as a clock hand: its direction says where the crest is (the phase). Adding waves is adding hands tip to tail, which is exactly what moving the token does. Hands that point the same way make a taller wave. Opposite hands cancel. Hands at right angles make a wave about 1.4 times taller.

Brightness is the wave's height squared. Put 3 ♥ and 4 ♠ on one detector and the brightness is 25, because 3² + 4² = 5². In quantum physics, this squared height is the chance of finding the photon there. Quantum computers use the same trick: they arrange for the waves of wrong answers to cancel.

Simplification. Real light keeps its total brightness fixed, so brightening one detector must dim the other. In this game both can grow. The four suits stand for four phases; real waves can have any phase.

Background. The arrow method comes from Feynman's QED (1985). Choudhary and Blair's "phasor wheels" (European Journal of Physics, 2021) use the same idea as a hands-on tool for middle school and up.
