How to play

Goal. Make your detector bright and your opponent's dark, then catch the photon.

Print the 40 wave cards ↗

Cards. The arrow gives the direction; the number is wave strength (amplitude) and tells you how many squares to move. ↑ up is blue, ↓ down red, → right purple, and ← left gold. There are 40 cards: seven strength-1 and three strength-2 cards per direction. The small plots all use the same scale and frequency.

Each round (8 rounds). Deal three cards each. Both players secretly choose a card and a detector: their own to build brightness, or their opponent’s to reduce it. Reveal both. Move each chosen detector by the full number of squares in the arrow’s direction, then refill hands. ↑2 and ↓1 leave ↑1; ↑2 and ↓2 cancel completely. Resolve Player A’s card first, then Player B’s.

Catch the photon. After 8 rounds, fire the photon. It lands at a random spot on the bar, and each detector's share of the bar equals its share of the total brightness. If both detectors are dark (0), play one more round.

Solo quick play. Click “Solo quick play” to show your hand and play against the computer. Each choice resolves both cards and starts the next round. “New game” keeps your play mode for an easy replay.

Passing one screen. Before each turn, the other player looks away, then the player clicks "Show my cards". On a projector, use "Open hands" so the class can discuss each move, or tick "Computer plays B".

Edge of the board. A card cannot push a token off the board. If both cards push the same token off the edge in one round, the second card is lost.

Why it works

The arrow is a direction on the amplitude board, not the photon’s travel direction. It represents the phase: where a wave starts in its cycle. The number gives its amplitude. Up starts at a crest, down at a trough, right at zero rising, and left at zero falling. Adding these waves means adding their arrows tip to tail.

In-step amplitudes add. Opposite phases subtract; equal opposites cancel completely. Two equal waves a quarter-cycle apart have a combined amplitude about 1.4 times either one. The plots show a wave value over time, not the path of a photon.

At board position (x, y), brightness is x² + y². A total of 3 squares right and 4 up gives 25. This is an unnormalized probability weight, not a probability by itself. If the other detector has brightness 9, the chances are 25/34 and 9/34. Dividing each weight by the total normalizes the probabilities. Quantum computers use interference to reduce unwanted outcomes and increase desired ones.

Simplification. In an ideal fixed-input, lossless two-output optical setup, the total detection probability is conserved: increasing one output’s probability decreases the other’s. This game instead lets you build two relative amplitudes independently and then normalizes their squared magnitudes. It does not simulate that optical apparatus. Real waves can have any phase; the cards use four.

Background. The arrow method comes from Feynman's QED (1985). Choudhary and Blair's "phasor wheels" (European Journal of Physics, 2021) use the same idea as a hands-on tool for middle school and up.
