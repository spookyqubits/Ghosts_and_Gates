    How to play

    The idea. A ghost hides in one of 8 rooms, numbered in binary from 000 to 111. A quantum computer spreads a search wave over every room (4 in each room). It cannot look at the wave, but it can change it.

    One round = flip, then reflect. 1. FLIP: the ghost room's wave changes sign (only the hider knows which room). 2. AVERAGE: add all 8 rooms and divide by 8. 3. REFLECT: every room's new wave = 2 × average − old wave. 4. CHECK: the squares of all 8 waves still add up to 128.

    Two numbers are enough. Every empty room always has the same wave, so you only track G (each ghost room) and E (each empty room). With M ghosts: average = (M × (−G) + (8 − M) × E) ÷ 8, new G = 2 × average + G, new E = 2 × average − E.

    Look. Flip 7 coins and read them as a binary number from 0 to 127. If it is less than M × G², you caught a ghost.

    Ghost Duel. The hider secretly picks 1, 2 or 3 ghost rooms. The hunter then picks 0 to 3 rounds without knowing how many ghosts there are. The game reveals the number of ghosts (not where they are), runs the rounds, and the hunter looks. Swap roles each night; 6 nights.

    Why it works

    This is Grover's quantum search algorithm (1996). The flip is the "oracle", which marks the answer. Reflecting around the average is the "diffusion" step. Each round moves a little more of the wave into the ghost room, but too many rounds push it back out again. With a million rooms the best number of rounds is 804, while a normal computer would need to open about half a million rooms. A real quantum computer does the flip and reflect with gates on qubits; here you do the same arithmetic by hand.

    Faithfulness. The two-number rule is exactly Grover's algorithm: it matches the real circuit (Hadamard gates, oracle, diffusion) for every hiding place. The 7-coin look matches the exact chance to within 0.6%.

    Background. Hadamard's Hoard (Institute for Quantum Computing, University of Waterloo) is an earlier quantum-search board game. In it a quantum player races a classical player along a track of preset chances. Ghost Hunt instead has students compute the waves themselves and adds the hidden number of ghosts.
